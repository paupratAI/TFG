
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

The work studied a Michigan-style evolutionary architecture for a
single-hidden-layer neural network. The central idea was to evolve hidden neurons
as individuals, form the hidden layer from the surviving population, and fit
the output layer separately. The final prototype keeps this hidden
representation fixed across tasks, but uses an output layer that matches the
problem: logistic cross-entropy for binary classification, Ridge/GCV for
regression, and softmax cross-entropy for multiclass classification.

\subsection{Assessment of the Objectives}
\label{subsec:conclusions_objectives}

The \textbf{first objective} was to implement a single-hidden-layer neural network in
which the hidden neurons are evolved with a Michigan-style Evolution Strategy.
This part of the work was completed. The final prototype defines each
individual as one tanh hidden neuron with its own genotype and self-adaptive
mutation matrix. The population collectively forms the hidden layer, and the
output layer is fitted after selection.

The \textbf{second objective} was to study credit assignment in this representation.
This was also addressed experimentally. Four selection strategies were
implemented and compared: OMP residual selection, marginal $R^2$ with
diversity penalty, CFS, and CFS-AUC. The experiments show that the selection
criterion has a large effect on performance. CFS is the strongest overall
strategy in the main binary logistic benchmark.

The \textbf{third objective} was to establish a task-consistent output formulation once the hidden layer had been selected. This was completed: the final prototype uses logistic cross-entropy for binary classification, Ridge/GCV for regression, and softmax cross-entropy for multiclass classification.

The \textbf{fourth objective} was to evaluate the binary logistic architecture against
standard baselines and ablations. The outcome is balanced rather than
one-sided. ES-CFS Logistic CE improves over the linear logistic and random tanh
logistic ablations, which supports the value of the evolved representation.
However, the strongest classical tabular baselines, especially RBF SVM and
Random Forest, still obtain higher average test accuracy in the tested binary
datasets. The conclusion is therefore not that the prototype dominates
classical models, but that the Michigan representation adds measurable value
inside the logistic neural architecture.

The \textbf{fifth objective} was to test related extensions. This was only partially
covered, but the results are still useful. The regression benchmark shows that
the evolved representation improves a closed-form Ridge/GCV output model over
linear and random-feature Ridge variants in the tested regression settings.
The L1-logistic experiment shows that the final hidden layer can be strongly
sparsified while preserving nearly the same test accuracy and improving
probability quality. The softmax benchmark shows that the evolved
representation can also be used in multiclass probabilistic classification.
These extensions are not exhaustive benchmark studies, but they add clear
value to the project.

The \textbf{sixth objective} was to compare the Michigan representation with a
Pittsburgh-style evolutionary representation. The final experiment addresses
this objective directly. Against full-network Pittsburgh evolution, Michigan
ES-CFS obtains higher test accuracy and ROC-AUC, a smaller validation-test
gap, and lower runtime in the tested setting. The matched-budget Pittsburgh
variant points in the same direction while sharing the output solver. This is
the result most directly aligned with the original motivation of the thesis.

\subsection{Main Technical Conclusions}
\label{subsec:conclusions_technical}

The first technical conclusion is that separating hidden-representation search
from output-layer fitting is a useful design. The ES searches for nonlinear
tanh neurons, while the output layer is fitted afterwards with a task-specific
model. This separates the architecture from both standard
backpropagation and conventional Pittsburgh neuroevolution.

The second conclusion is that the Michigan representation is meaningful only
if the neuron-level fitness strategy is handled carefully. A population of
neurons is not automatically a good hidden layer; the selection strategy
determines whether the algorithm keeps complementary neurons or wastes
capacity on redundancy.

The third conclusion is that the output layer should not be treated as a
minor implementation detail. Ridge/GCV is very useful for regression because
it gives an analytical and automatically regularised output solve. For
classification, logistic and softmax outputs are more appropriate because
they produce probabilities and can be evaluated with cross-entropy,
calibration error, and ROC-AUC. The price is that the closed-form GCV
advantage is lost in the probabilistic classifiers.

The Michigan--Pittsburgh comparison gives the clearest support for the
original idea. Under the tested setting, evolving individual neurons gives a
better accuracy-time trade-off than evolving complete neural networks. The
matched-budget Pittsburgh variant supports the same interpretation from a
more isolated perspective. This does not make the method a universal benchmark
winner, but it does show that the architecture is coherent and empirically
defensible.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:conclusions_limitations}

The clearest limitation is the scale of the evaluation. The experiments are
reproducible and cover several directions, but they are still small compared
with a full benchmark study. More datasets and more seeds would be needed before making broad claims.

A second limitation is experimental scope. Regression, sparse logistic
filtering, and softmax are evaluated as targeted extensions rather than as a
single unified modelling framework. The Pittsburgh comparison is also a
specific baseline, not a judgement on every possible Pittsburgh algorithm.
The matched-budget Pittsburgh variant helps isolate representation, but it is not the main
Pittsburgh definition used in the thesis.

Probability quality also remains an issue in the logistic variants. The
unregularised logistic output can reach good accuracy but sometimes becomes
overconfident when the selected hidden representation is nearly separable.
This motivates further work on calibration, regularised logistic output
layers, or early-stopping criteria based directly on probabilistic metrics.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:conclusions_future}

Future work should first expand the benchmark to more OpenML datasets and
more seeds. A broader evaluation would make the empirical conclusions stronger
and allow paired comparisons between Michigan, Pittsburgh, standard baselines,
and ablations.

A second direction is to integrate binary logistic classification, Ridge/GCV
regression, softmax classification, and sparse logistic filtering into one
cleaner training framework. A third direction is to study neuron stability:
which neurons survive across seeds, which neurons are removed by the sparse
output layer, and whether active neurons correspond to meaningful feature
interactions.
The computational cost could also be analysed more formally in terms of
dataset size, hidden width, offspring count, selection cost, and number of
output fits.

\subsection{Final Remark}
\label{subsec:conclusions_final_remark}

The project began with a simple question: can a neural hidden layer be evolved
as a population of individual neurons rather than as a complete network? The
answer from this prototype is cautiously positive. The method is not a
universal replacement for backpropagation or for classical tabular models, but
it is a coherent and competitive architecture. It shows that a Michigan-style
representation can be combined with a one-hidden-layer MLP and task-specific
output fitting, and that this combination can offer a meaningful
accuracy-time trade-off compared with evolving complete neural networks.
